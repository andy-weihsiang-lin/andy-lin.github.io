When it comes to the time to “do”, how do you decide what to do at any given point?

9.1 The Four-Criteria Model for Choosing Actions in the Moment

Make your action choices based on the following four criteria:

  • Context
  • Time available
  • Energy available
  • Priority

9.1.1 Context

If you have a bunch of things to do on one to-do list, but you actually cannot do many of them in the same context, you force yourself to continually keep reconsidering all of them.

Therefore, at any point in time, the first thing to consider is,

  • what could you possibly do, where you are, with the tool you have?
    • i.e., what are the current restrictions that limit the action choices?

See Section 7.2.2 for details of context categories.

What can help:

  • Organize your action reminder by context
  • Look for actions that suit the current context

9.1.2 Time Available

A total-life action reminder inventory will

  • give you maximum information about what you need to do, and
  • make it much easier to match your action to the windows you have.

Therefore, next thing to consider is

  • how much time you have before your have to do something else?

What can help:

  • Keep a list of small things that can be completed in a shorter term (e.g., ten-minute)
  • Look for actions that fits the current timeframe

9.1.3 Energy Available

  • The tail end of a day taken up mostly by a marathon budget-planning session is not the best time to call a prospective client.
  • It is better to call the airline to change a reservation, process some expense receipts, or skim a trade journal.
  • One of the best ways to increase your energy is to close some of your loops.

What can help:

  • Keep an inventory of things that needs to be done that require very little mental or creative horsepower
  • Look for actions that require the right level of energy available

9.1.4 Priority

Given the (1) context you are in and the (2) time and (3) energy you have, the obvious next criteria for action choice is the (4) relative priority.

9.2 The Threefold Model for Evaluating Daily Work

At any point in time, you will be engaged in one of the three:

  • Doing predefined work
  • Doing work as it shows up
  • Defining your work

Many people

  • let themselves get wrapped around the second activity much too easily; and
  • let the other two slide, to their detriment.

However, for most professional, the nature of their job requires us to be instantly available to handle new work as it appears in many forms. So, when can we allow those new priorities (ad-hoc work) to override?

You can do only one of these work activities at a time, so it is acceptable if you are

  • confident about what you have decided to do
  • conscious of what you are doing

But again, how to ensure that?

9.3 The Six-Level Model for Reviewing Your Own Work

There are six levels of work that can be thought of in terms of altitude:

  • 50,000+ feet: Life
  • 40,000 feet: Three- to five-year visions
  • 30,000 feet: One- to two-year goals
  • 20,000 feet: Areas of responsibility
  • 10,000 feet: Current projects
  • Runway: Current actions

Ensure that each of these levels should enhance and align with the ones above it.

  • E.g., If your job structure doesn’t match up with where you need to be a year from now, you should rethink how you are framed your areas of focus and responsibilities.

The most efficient prioritization method: Start with the tasks from lower levels (i.e., using the bottom-up method)

Why not work from the top down?

  • There is a pattern of failure in the top-down approach.
    • The top-down approach always creates the absences of actions, and that absence creates frustration.
  • And why is there frustration?
    • Because there is nothing we can manage or do from the top.
    • Yet we can manage low-level actions only.
