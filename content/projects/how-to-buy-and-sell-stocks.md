## 1 What is a Stock?

A stock is a small ownership stake in a company.

When you buy shares (股份) of a company, you become one of its owners. For example:
- A company is divided into 1,000,000 shares.
- You buy 100 shares.
- You then own 100/1,000,000, which is 1/10,000, fraction of that company.

As an owner, you may benenfit if
- The company become more valuable.
   - So the fractions (i.e., shares) become more valuable, too.
- The company pays dividends (股利).

## 2 How Are Stock Prices Generated?

Imagine there is a marketplace where people buy and sell shares.

This marketplace is called **stock exchange** (股票交易所).

At a specific moment:

| Buyers | &emsp;Price&emsp; | Sellers |
| :---: |:---:| :---: |
|  | $25 |  |
|  | $24 | Seller #533 wants to sell 1 share |
|  | $23 | Seller #228 wants to sell 1 share |
|  | $22 | Seller #141 wants to sell 1 share |
|  | $21 |  |
|  | $20 |  |
| Buyer #802 wants to buy 1 share | $19 |  |
| Buyer #614 wants to buy 1 share | $18 |  |
| Buyer #431 wants to buy 1 share | $17 |  |
| Buyer #638 wants to buy 1 share | $16 |  |
|  | $15 |  |

**No trade happens** because:
- All buyers want to buy at lower prices (i.e., no one is willing to buy at a higher price)
- All sellers want to sell at higher prices (i.e., no one is willing to sell at a lower price)

### The Order Book
The stock exchange maintains a list of outstanding orders.

#### Bid (買)
The highest price someone is willing to pay.

Example:
- There are three buyers trying to buy at $19, $18, $17, and $16, respectively.
- And the highest bid is $19.

#### Ask (賣)
The lowest price someone is willing to accept.

Example:
- There are three seller trying to sell at $22, $23 and $24, respectively.
- And the lowest ask is $22.

A trade occurs when a buyer and seller agree.

### What Is the Stock Price?
After the trade: **Price = $22**

Why? Because $22 was the price of the most recent transaction (交易).

Note:
- The company did not determine this price.
- The exchange did not determine this price.
- The market participants determine this price through their bids and asks.

Suppose exciting news appears.

More people want to buy this stock.

The order book may become:

| Buyers | &emsp;Price&emsp; | Sellers |
| :---: |:---:| :---: |
|  | $25 |  |
|  | $24 | Seller #533 wants to sell 1 share |
| <span style="color: green;">Buyer #802 wants to buy 1 share</span> | $23 | Seller #228 wants to sell 1 share |
| <span style="color: green;">Buyer #436 wants to buy 1 share</span> | $22 |  |
| <span style="color: green;">Buyer #846 wants to buy 1 share</span> | $21 |  |
| <span style="color: green;">Buyer #874 wants to buy 1 share</span> | $20 |  |
| Buyer #802 wants to buy 1 share | $19 |  |

- So now buyers are willing to pay more.
- The next trade occurs at a higher price $23, the **market price**.
- The stock price rises.

### Why Does the Price Move Down?
Suppose investors become pessimistic.

The order book may become:

| Buyers | &emsp;Price&emsp; | Sellers |
| :---: |:---:| :---: |
|  | $25 |  |
|  | $24 | Seller #533 wants to sell 1 share |
|  | $23 | Seller #228 wants to sell 1 share |
|  | $22 | <span style="color: green;">Seller #210 wants to sell 1 share</span> |
|  | $21 | <span style="color: green;">Seller #356 wants to sell 1 share</span> |
|  | $20 | <span style="color: green;">Seller #233 wants to sell 1 share</span> |
| <span style="color: green;">(Buyer #802 cancelled his order)</span> | $19 | <span style="color: green;">Seller #753 wants to sell 1 share</span> |
| Buyer #614 wants to buy 1 share | $18 | <span style="color: green;">Seller #370 wants to sell 1 share</span> |
| Buyer #431 wants to buy 1 share | $17 |  |
| Buyer #638 wants to buy 1 share | $16 |  |
|  | $15 |  |

- Trades now occur at lower prices.
- The next trade occurs at a lower price $18.
- The stock price falls.

### A Deeper Insight
The stock price is not the company's value.

The **market price** of a stock is the **latest consensus between buyers and sellers**.

The company's **intrinsic value** is *the value of the future cash the business can generate*, but it may not be identical to its market price.
- A wonderful company can become temporarily overpriced.
- A wonderful company can become temporarily underpriced, too.
- A struggling company can become temporarily underpriced.
- A struggling company can become temporarily overpriced, too.
