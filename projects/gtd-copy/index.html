This page serves as a checklist for each stage of Getting Things Done in my case.

1 Getting Started

1.1 Set Aside the Time

Create a block of time to initialize the preparation of workstation.

Ideal time frame: Two whole days, back to back.

  • For most professional: weekend or holiday .
  • If interruptions can be kept to minimum: Typical workday .

1.2 Set up the Space

1.2.1 Main

  • Establish an office space.
  • Install a desk.
  • If you work from a home office, establish a satellite system.

1.2.2 Basics

  • A writing suface
  • Room for an in-basket

1.2.3 Optional

  • A phone
  • A computer
  • A printer
  • Stacking trays
  • File drawers
  • Reference shelves

1.3 Get the Tools You’ll Need

  • Paper holding trays
  • A stack of plain letter-size paper
  • Pen and pencil
  • Post-its (3x3s), paper clips, stapler, scotch tape, rubber bands
  • An automatic labeler
  • File folders
  • A calendar
  • Wastebasket and recycle bins

2 Collection: Corralling Your Stuff

2.1 Look for Open Loops

Open loops are what we are looking for:

  • All incomplete items with features “should”, “need to”, or “ought to”.
  • Anything that is “pending”, “in-progess”, or “not-yet-finished”.

We will move all open loops into an in-basket.

2.2 Warning

  • Do NOT purge.
  • Go for quantity.

2.3 Where to Find Open Loops?

Here is a comprehensive checklist of collection points: click here.

3 Process

  • Goal
    • To get to the bottom of the in-basket
  • Method
    • FIFO process one thing at a time
  • Warning
    • Do NOT multi-tasking
    • Do NOT put things back to the in-basket

3.1 Rule

  • if there is no required next action:
    • if it has no future value (i.e., it istrash):
      • Toss out immediately
    • if it may trigger new action later:
      • Write it down on the “Someday/Maybe” list
    • if it has potentially useful information:
      • Keep it as reference
        • Label it
        • Put it in a file folder
        • Tuck into your filing drawer
  • elif there is required next action:
    • You are the most appropriate person to do it:
      • If the action takes <= 2 minutes:
        • Do it now, regardless of priority or impact
      • If the action takes > 2 minutes:
        • Defer it
        • Add it to “Pending” list
    • You are NOT the most appropriate person to do it:
      • Delegate it via Email (fastest, e-record)
      • Track it via the “Waiting For” list

4 Organize: Setting Up the Right Buckets

4.1 Category Establishment

  • A “Projects” List
  • Project support material
  • Calendar actions and information
  • A “Next Actions” List
  • A “Waiting For” List
  • Reference material
  • A “Someday/Maybe” List

Note: Lists don’t need to be assigned priorities.

4.2 Organizing Action Reminders

4.2.1 Day- or Time-Specific Actions

When you make any appointment, write it into your calendar and trust your calendar.

4.2.2 ASAP Actions

Categorize these actions by context. Some examples include:

  • By Tool
    • “At Computer”
    • “Errand (i.e., need a car)”
  • By Person
    • “Boss”
    • “Partner”
    • “Your Attorney”
  • By Meeting
    • “Staff Meeting”
    • “Project Meeting”
  • By Place
    • “At Home”
    • “At Office”

4.3 Organizing “Waiting For”

Keep the “Waiting For” list close at hand.

  • It is a trigger about what you are waiting for from whom

4.4 Organizing Projects Reminders

Create and maintain the “Projects” List

  • Personal projects
  • Professional projects
  • Delegated projects

4.5 Organizing Project Support Materials

Do NOT use support material for reminding

4.6 Organizing Ad Hoc Thinking

Use attached notes, Email, database, paper-based files, or notebooks

4.7 Organizagin Non-Actionable Data

4.7.1 Reference System

  • Install one General-Reference Filing (and only one)
  • Install a separate Large-Category Filing for any topic that requires more then 50 file folders
  • Use Rolodexes for your network
    • Home and office phone numbers, addresses, cell-phone numbers, fax numbers, email addresses
    • Birthdays, names of friends’ and colleagues family members, hobbies, favorite wines and foods, etc.
  • Build a Library and Archives for things you think will need in the meetings, trips, office, etc.

4.7.2 “Someday/Maybe” List

  • Install your own tickler system
    • Do NOT share it with others
  • Update it every day
  • Make an inventory of your creative imaginings:
    • Things to get or build for your home
    • Hobbies to take up
    • Skills to learn
    • Creative expression to explore
    • Clothes and accessories to buy
    • Toys and gears to acquire
    • Trips to take
    • Organizations to join
    • Service projects to contribute to
    • Things to see and do
  • Categories can include:
    • Food: Recipes, menus, restaurants, wines
    • Family: Things to do with them
    • Books
    • CDs
    • Videos
    • Cultural events
    • Gift ideas
    • Garden ideas
    • Websites to surf
    • Weekend trips
    • Meeting ideas
    • Party ideas

4.8 Organizing Checklists

4.8.1 How/Why Do You Need Checklists?

  • The need for checklists depends on your familiarity to the tasks.
  • The more novel the situation, the more control (checklists) is required.

4.8.2 When Do You Need Checlists?

A two-dimensional method:

  • By Areas of Commitments (i.e., work)
    • Career goals
    • Service
    • Family
    • Relationships
    • Community
    • Heath and energy
    • Financial resources
    • Creative expression
  • By Areas of Responsibility
    • Team morale
    • Processes
    • Timelines
    • Staff issues
    • Workload
    • Communication

4.8.3 Develop Checklists at All Levels

  • Personal Affirmations
    • Personal value statements
  • Job Area of Responsibility
  • Travel
    • Everything to take on or do before a trip
  • Weekly Review
    • Everything to review and/or update on a weekly basis
  • Training Program Components
    • All things to handle when putting on an event, front to back
  • Conference Checklist
    • Everything to handle when putting on a conference
  • Focus Areas
    • Key life roles and responsibilities
  • Key People in My Life/Work
    • Relationships to assess regularly for completion and opportunity development
  • Organization Chart
    • Key people and areas of output to manage and maintain
  • Personal Development
    • Things to evaluate regularly to ensure personal balance and progress

5 Review

Block out two hours early Friday afternoon to weekly review the system.

  • The events of the weekly are still fresh enough for you to be able to do a complete postmortem.
  • When uncovering actions that requires reaching colleagues, you still have time to do that before they leave for the weekend.
  • It is great to clear your psychic deck so you can go into the weekend ready for refreshment and recreation.

Targets are the following:

  • Calendar
    • Previous data
    • Upcoming events
  • “Projects” lists
    • Evaluate the status of projects, goals, and outcomes one by one
  • “Next Actions” lists
    • Mark off completed actions
    • Review reminders
  • “Waiting For” list
    • Record appropriate actions for any needed follow-up
    • Check off received items
  • Checklists
  • “Someday/Maybe” list
    • Check for any projects that may have been active
    • Transfer them to “Projects” list
    • Delete items no longer of interest
  • “Pending” and Support Files

6 Do

First question: When it comes to the time to “do”, how do you decide what to do at any given point?

6.1 The Four-Criteria Model for Choosing Actions in the Moment

Make your action choices based on the following four criteria:

  • Context
  • Time available
  • Energy available
  • Priority

6.1.1 Context

If you have a bunch of things to do on one to-do list, but you actually cannot do many of them in the same context, you force yourself to continually keep reconsidering all of them.

Therefore, at any point in time, the first thing to consider is,

  • what could you possibly do, where you are, with the tool you have?
    • i.e., what are the current restrictions that limit the action choices?

See Section 4.2.2 for details of context categories.

What can help:

  • Organize your action reminder by context
  • Look for actions that suit the current context

6.1.2 Time Available

A total-life action reminder inventory will

  • give you maximum information about what you need to do, and
  • make it much easier to match your action to the windows you have.

Therefore, next thing to consider is

  • how much time you have before your have to do something else?

What can help:

  • Keep a list of small things that can be completed in a shorter term (e.g., ten-minute)
  • Look for actions that fits the current timeframe

6.1.3 Energy Available

  • The tail end of a day taken up mostly by a marathon budget-planning session is not the best time to call a prospective client.
  • It is better to call the airline to change a reservation, process some expense receipts, or skim a trade journal.
  • One of the best ways to increase your energy is to close some of your loops.

What can help:

  • Keep an inventory of things that needs to be done that require very little mental or creative horsepower
  • Look for actions that require the right level of energy available

6.1.4 Priority

Given the (1) context you are in and the (2) time and (3) energy you have, the obvious next criteria for action choice is the (4) relative priority.

6.2 The Threefold Model for Evaluating Daily Work

At any point in time, you will be engaged in one of the three:

  • Doing predefined work
  • Doing work as it shows up
  • Defining your work

Many people

  • let themselves get wrapped around the second activity much too easily; and
  • let the other two slide, to their detriment.

However, for most professional, the nature of their job requires us to be instantly available to handle new work as it appears in many forms. So, when can we allow those new priorities (ad-hoc work) to override?

You can do only one of these work activities at a time, so it is acceptable if you are

  • confident about what you have decided to do
  • conscious of what you are doing

But again, how to ensure that?

6.3 The Six-Level Model for Reviewing Your Own Work

There are six levels of work that can be thought of in terms of altitude:

  • 50,000+ feet: Life
  • 40,000 feet: Three- to five-year visions
  • 30,000 feet: One- to two-year goals
  • 20,000 feet: Areas of responsibility
  • 10,000 feet: Current projects
  • Runway: Current actions

Ensure that each of these levels should enhance and align with the ones above it.

  • E.g., If your job structure doesn’t match up with where you need to be a year from now, you should rethink how you are framed your areas of focus and responsibilities.

The most efficient prioritization method: Start with the tasks from lower levels (i.e., using the bottom-up method)

Why not work from the top down?

  • There is a pattern of failure in the top-down approach.
    • The top-down approach always creates the absences of actions, and that absence creates frustration.
  • And why is there frustration?
    • Because there is nothing we can manage or do from the top.
    • Yet we can manage low-level actions only.
